Why measure a voice over time

A single reading says little. Voices differ from one person to the next, and from morning to evening in the same person, so one number on its own tells you very little about you.

Change against your own earlier readings says much more. What matters is how your voice compares with your own usual, not with an average of other people, which is why this page gives no norms and no verdicts.

Readings are only comparable when they are taken the same way: the same text, the same microphone, the same room and distance. That is why I suggest one passage, and why it is worth reading the same one each time.

Researchers have found that the voice carries signals about the heart. In a study of more than 10,000 people, a voice score built from hundreds of acoustic features was linked to hospital stays and deaths among people with heart failure[1]. A Mayo Clinic study that followed patients for two years found that people with a high voice score were more likely to go on to have heart problems, including heart attacks[2]. Those scores combine many measurements into one; no single number on this page does that job by itself.

Stroke is different. Slurred or difficult speech is one of the main warning signs of a stroke that is happening right now[3]. I am not aware of evidence that voice measurements can predict a stroke before it starts. What they can do is notice a sudden change from someone’s normal voice, and with a stroke, minutes matter.

That is why SeniorMinder compares each senior with their own history rather than with an average.

What to watch for, and why

One reading on its own tells you very little. Voices vary a lot from person to person, so the comparison that matters is with your own earlier readings, and how quickly things change. A sudden change over hours or days points to something happening now. A slow drift over weeks or months points to something gradual. None of these numbers diagnoses anything. Each one is a reason to look closer.

What I would watch most closely

  • A sudden change in several readings at once, especially speaking rate, pauses and voice clarity. That pattern says check on them now, and it is what separates something serious from a bad day.
  • A slow slide in pitch variation, loudness and speaking rate together over weeks. That is the pattern of low mood, fatigue or a progressing neurological condition.
  • Negative words and a flatter voice together. Mood shows in what is said and in how it is said, and the two together mean more than either alone.
